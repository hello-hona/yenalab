North America

From northern lands to tropical islands

North America stretches from icy lands near the Arctic to islands in warm tropical seas. Tall mountains rise in the west, and wide grassy plains fill the middle.

The continent includes the broad north of Canada and the United States, the narrowing lands of Mexico and Central America, and the Caribbean islands. Western mountains, central plains and old eastern highlands follow one another.

Mountain belts in the west

Several mountain belts, including the Rockies and coastal ranges, run along the west. The continent’s highest peak is Denali in Alaska, at 6,194 m.

Source: USGS, Physiographic regions and provinces of the conterminous United States · National Geographic Society, North America: Physical Geography

The great central plains

The Great Plains spread east of the Rockies. Farther east, the Mississippi flows about 3,730 km to the Gulf of Mexico.

Source: USGS, Physiographic regions and provinces of the conterminous United States · National Geographic Society, North America: Physical Geography

Central America and the Caribbean

The land narrows southward until, in Panama, it is only about 50 km across. The Caribbean holds more than 7,000 islands, islets, reefs and cays.

Source: National Geographic Society, North America: Physical Geography · Natural Earth, Land, 1:50m

Northern America

Western mountains, interior plains and eastern highlands

Canada and the United States have tall mountains in the west, wide grasslands in the middle and lower mountains in the east. The north has very old rocky land and many lakes.

Across Canada and the United States, western mountains, interior plains, the ancient northern shield and the eastern Appalachians form distinct zones.

Rockies and coastal ranges

The Rockies run inland from British Columbia in Canada to New Mexico in the United States. Separate coastal ranges rise nearer the Pacific.

Source: USGS, Physiographic regions and provinces of the conterminous United States · National Geographic Society, North America: Physical Geography

Great Plains and the Mississippi

East of the Rockies, the Great Plains are covered in tall and short grass prairies. Farther east, the Mississippi, about 3,730 km long, crosses the interior lowlands.

Source: USGS, Physiographic regions and provinces of the conterminous United States · National Geographic Society, North America: Physical Geography

Ancient rock and eastern mountains

The Canadian Shield in the north exposes very ancient rock. Glaciers scoured it and left countless lakes. To the east, the Appalachians have been worn round by long erosion.

Source: USGS, About the Geologic Map in the National Atlas · National Geographic Society, North America: Physical Geography

Canada A maple-leaf flag · Niagara Falls. United States The Statue of Liberty · one of the most famous countries in the world.

Central America

From the broad Mexican Plateau to narrow Panama

Central America is like a land bridge between North and South America. It gets narrower toward the south and has rows of volcanoes.

This map spans Mexico to Panama: the broad Mexican Plateau in the north, the narrow isthmus to the south and a chain of volcanoes toward the Pacific.

Mexico’s plateau and ranges

A broad plateau fills northern and central Mexico, flanked by the Sierra Madre ranges. Some of their volcanoes rise above 5,600 m.

Source: NASA Earth Observatory, A Sliver of Mexico’s Mother Mountain Range · National Geographic Society, North America: Physical Geography

The Pacific volcanic belt

Volcanoes line the Pacific side from Guatemala toward Panama. They formed where an ocean plate sinks beneath the land plate.

Source: NASA Earth Observatory, San Salvador: A City Among Volcanoes

A narrow land between two seas

Toward the south the land narrows between the Pacific and the Caribbean. At Panama it is only about 50 km wide before joining South America.

Source: National Geographic Society, North America: Physical Geography · Natural Earth, Land, 1:50m

Mexico The land of the Maya civilization · tacos and cactuses.

Caribbean

Large islands, volcanic islands and limestone platforms

The Caribbean has many big and small islands. Some are large and mountainous, some were made by volcanoes, and some are low islands of coral and sand.

The Caribbean holds more than 7,000 islands, islets, reefs and cays, which differ in how they formed and how high they rise as well as in size.

The Greater Antilles

Cuba, Hispaniola, Jamaica and Puerto Rico are the large islands of the northern Caribbean, with mountains inland and plains along the coasts.

Source: USGS, Geologic map of the Greater Antilles and Virgin Islands · Natural Earth, Land, 1:50m

A chain of volcanic islands

The Lesser Antilles to the east form a curved chain of small islands. Many are volcanoes built where the Atlantic plate sinks beneath the Caribbean plate.

Source: USGS, Porphyry copper assessment of Central America and the Caribbean Basin

The low Bahamas

The Bahamas sit on shallow banks built from limy sediment such as shell and coral fragments. The islands are low and flat, surrounded by clear, shallow water.

Source: NASA Earth Observatory, Tidal Flats and Channels, Long Island, Bahamas

Cuba The largest island country in the Caribbean · rumba and the world's smallest bird. Jamaica The island where reggae was born · a stair-step waterfall that pours into the sea.
