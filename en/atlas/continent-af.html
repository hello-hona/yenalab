Africa

Deserts and forests across the equator

The equator runs through the middle of Africa. The Sahara, the world’s largest hot desert, covers the north. Thick forests grow near the equator, and tall mountains rise in the east.

The equator divides Africa almost in half, so similar climate belts appear to its north and south. Broad plateaus and deserts, rainforest river basins and eastern rifts and volcanoes all occur.

The Sahara and the Sahel

The Sahara covers about 8.5 million km², roughly a quarter of Africa. Along its southern edge, the Sahel is a belt of light rain between desert and grassland.

Source: National Geographic Society, Africa: Physical Geography · NASA Earth Observatory, Vegetation and Rainfall in the Sahel

The equatorial Congo Basin

Heavy rain near the equator feeds wide rainforest in the Congo Basin. North and south of it lie savannas of tall grass and scattered trees.

Source: NASA Earth Observatory, Cloudy Congo River Basin · National Geographic Society, Africa: Physical Geography

Eastern rifts and high peaks

In East Africa the crust is stretching apart, opening long, deep rift valleys and volcanoes. Kilimanjaro, Africa’s highest point at 5,895 m, is a volcano.

Source: USGS, Seismicity of the Earth 1900-2013: East African Rift · UNESCO, Kilimanjaro National Park

Regions

Regions

North Africa

The Sahara, the Atlas Mountains and the Nile

Most of North Africa is the Sahara Desert. Only along the Nile, which crosses the desert, does a long green strip appear.

The region combines Mediterranean shores, the Atlas Mountains in the northwest, the dry Sahara and the fertile lands along the Nile.

More gravel than sand

About 70 percent of the Sahara is gravel plain, and sand dune seas cover about 20 percent. Some dunes rise more than 300 m high.

Source: National Geographic Society, Africa: Physical Geography

The Atlas Mountains

The Atlas Mountains stretch from Morocco through Algeria to Tunisia. They block moist Mediterranean air, so the coast to the north and the Sahara to the south have very different climates.

Source: NASA Earth Observatory, Topography of Western Algeria · Natural Earth, Land, 1:50m

The Nile through the desert

The Nile rises in highlands to the south and crosses the deserts of Sudan and Egypt to the Mediterranean. Farmland forms a green ribbon along its banks and delta.

Source: NASA Earth Observatory, Nile Bend, Egypt · National Geographic Society, Africa: Physical Geography

Egypt Pyramids and the Sphinx · the Nile River. Morocco The gateway to the Sahara · the blue city of Chefchaouen.

East Africa

Rift highlands and Indian Ocean islands

In East Africa the ground is slowly splitting apart. Long, deep lakes have filled the cracks. Kilimanjaro, Africa’s highest mountain, is here too.

The region holds the Ethiopian Highlands, lakes and volcanoes along the rifts, the Indian Ocean coast and the island of Madagascar.

The East African Rift

The East African Rift is a chain of long valleys formed as the crust pulls apart. Volcanoes line the rifts, and most of Africa’s tallest mountains stand in the Ethiopian Highlands.

Source: USGS, Seismicity of the Earth 1900-2013: East African Rift · National Geographic Society, Africa: Physical Geography

Deep rift lakes

Lakes that fill rift valleys, such as Tanganyika, are long, narrow and very deep. Lake Victoria instead sits in a shallow basin between two branches of the rift, so it is broad and round.

Source: USGS, Seismicity of the Earth 1900-2013: East African Rift

Kilimanjaro and Madagascar

Kilimanjaro, at 5,895 m, is Africa’s highest volcano. Out in the Indian Ocean lies the large island of Madagascar, where rivers run from its central highlands to the sea.

Source: UNESCO, Kilimanjaro National Park · NASA Earth Observatory, Madagascar’s Muddy Rivers

Kenya The land of safaris · the Maasai Mara grasslands.

Southern Africa

Dry west, inland highlands and major rivers

The middle of southern Africa is high, flat land. A red sand desert lies along the west coast. The mighty Victoria Falls drops a great river over a cliff.

The region joins the dry Namib coast, the inland Kalahari basin, high plateaus and eastern mountains, and the Zambezi River. It also holds some of Earth’s oldest rocks.

Namib and Kalahari

The Namib is a desert of dunes along the Atlantic coast. Inland, the Kalahari is sandy too, but grass and shrubs grow over much of it.

Source: NASA Earth Observatory, Sandscapes of the Namib Desert · National Geographic Society, Africa: Physical Geography

Ancient plateau and eastern mountains

The interior of southern Africa is a broad plateau resting on the Kaapvaal craton, rock more than 2.6 billion years old. The Drakensberg rises along its eastern edge.

Source: National Geographic Society, Africa: Physical Geography

Zambezi and Victoria Falls

Between Zambia and Zimbabwe, the Zambezi plunges over Victoria Falls, about 1,700 m wide. Below, the river has cut deep zigzag gorges.

Source: UNESCO, Mosi-oa-Tunya / Victoria Falls

South Africa The rainbow nation · the Cape of Good Hope.

Central Africa

Congo forests and drier northern basins

Central Africa has the largest rainforest in Africa. The great Congo River winds through the trees. To the north, the land grows drier.

The region spans the rivers and forests of the Congo Basin, the drier Chad Basin to the north, and the grasslands and plateaus between them.

Forests of the Congo Basin

The Congo Basin is a wide, bowl-shaped lowland. Heavy equatorial rain feeds dense rainforest, and many rivers gather into the Congo.

Source: NASA Earth Observatory, Cloudy Congo River Basin

The Chad Basin to the north

The Chad Basin to the north lies in the dry Sahel, a belt about 5,400 km long from Senegal to Sudan. Lake Chad grows and shrinks greatly with the rains.

Source: NASA Earth Observatory, The Ups and Downs of Lake Chad · National Geographic Society, Africa: Physical Geography

The Congo reaches the sea

The Congo sweeps in a great arc across its basin before reaching the Atlantic. Near the coast it passes through narrow gorges and rapids.

Source: National Geographic Society, Africa: Physical Geography · Natural Earth, Land, 1:50m

Cameroon Called "Africa in miniature" · a green, red and yellow flag.

West Africa

From the Sahara’s edge to the Gulf of Guinea

West Africa gets drier toward the north and wetter toward the southern coast. From desert to grassland to forest, the landscape changes in stripes.

The region runs from the Sahara and Sahel in the north to savannas and humid coastal forests in the south, with the Niger River basin between.

Landscapes in stripes

The Sahel, along the Sahara’s southern edge, is a belt about 5,400 km long from Senegal to Sudan. Rainfall increases southward, bringing savanna and then forest.

Source: NASA Earth Observatory, Vegetation and Rainfall in the Sahel · National Geographic Society, Africa: Physical Geography

An inland delta

In Mali, the Niger spreads into many channels across inland lowlands, forming wide wetlands long before it reaches the sea. In the rainy season the flooded area grows enormously.

Source: NASA Earth Observatory, Inland Niger Delta, Mali

Atlantic coast and Gulf of Guinea

The Atlantic coast curves east along the south into the Gulf of Guinea. Its shores are rainy, with broad forests and wetlands.

Source: Natural Earth, Land, 1:50m · NASA Earth Observatory, Vegetation and Rainfall in the Sahel

Ghana The land of cacao and kente cloth · a flag with a black star. Nigeria Africa's most populous country · talking drums and jollof rice.
