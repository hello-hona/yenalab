Asia

From the roof of the world to northern forests

Asia is the largest continent in the world. Mount Everest, the highest mountain on Earth, is in Asia. It has high mountains, wide deserts and huge forests.

Asia covers most of Eurasia and about 30 percent of Earth’s land. Great rivers flow outward from its high central mountains and plateaus, building plains and deltas.

Himalayas and the Tibetan Plateau

The Himalayas rose as the Indian plate pushed into the Eurasian plate. Mount Everest, at 8,848 m, is the highest mountain on Earth. North of it, the Tibetan Plateau averages more than 4,500 m above sea level.

Source: National Geographic Society, Asia: Physical Geography · UNESCO, Sagarmatha National Park

Dry lands far from the sea

Moist sea air rarely reaches the continent’s interior, so rain is scarce. The Gobi has wide stretches of rock and gravel, while the Taklamakan and the Rub’ al Khali of Arabia are seas of sand dunes.

Source: NASA Earth Observatory, Gobi Dust Storm · NASA Earth Observatory, Dust and Clouds over the Taklimakan Desert · National Geographic Society, Asia: Physical Geography

Northern forests and plains

Siberia, across the top of the map, holds vast cold-tolerant conifer forests (taiga) and low plains. More than half of the West Siberian Plain lies under 100 m above sea level.

Source: National Geographic Society, Asia: Physical Geography

Regions

Regions

East Asia

From western highlands to eastern seas

East Asia is high in the west and low in the east. Big rivers start in the highlands and flow to the eastern seas. Along the coast are peninsulas and islands such as Korea and Japan.

This region joins the broad interiors of China and Mongolia with the coasts and islands of Korea, Japan and Taiwan. Land steps down from west to east.

High west, low east

Western China holds the Tibetan Plateau and high ranges, and the land falls to broad plains toward the east. The Yangtze, Asia’s longest river at about 6,300 km, runs from the plateau to the East China Sea.

Source: National Geographic Society, Asia: Physical Geography

The Gobi of Mongolia and China

The Gobi stretches across southern Mongolia and northern China. Much of it is rock and gravel plain, and strong spring winds can carry its dust far away.

Source: NASA Earth Observatory, Gobi Dust Storm

Peninsulas and island coasts

The Korean Peninsula reaches south from the mainland, while Japan is made of four large islands and many small ones. Taiwan is an island off southeastern mainland China.

Source: Natural Earth, Land, 1:50m

South Korea K-pop, taekwondo, and kimchi · an East Asian peninsula country that invented its own alphabet, Hangul. Japan An island country · sushi and Mount Fuji. China One of the most populous countries in the world · the Great Wall. Mongolia A land of grasslands and nomads · life in a ger.

Southeast Asia

River peninsulas and island groups

Southeast Asia has big peninsulas and thousands of islands. It lies near the equator, so it is warm and rainy all year. It also has many volcanoes.

The region divides into mainland peninsulas and island nations such as Indonesia and the Philippines. Low deltas sit at river mouths, while peninsula interiors and large islands hold mountains and volcanoes.

Mainland and islands

The Indochinese Peninsula, home to Vietnam and Thailand, is joined to the mainland, while Indonesia and the Philippines are island nations. Shallow seas and narrow straits lie between the islands.

Source: Natural Earth, Land, 1:50m

The Mekong and its delta

The Mekong begins on the Tibetan Plateau and crosses several countries before reaching the South China Sea. Silt carried by the river has built a wide, low delta in southern Vietnam.

Source: Mekong River Commission, Geography

Volcanoes where plates meet

Off Sumatra and Java, an ocean plate sinks beneath the continental plate, building lines of volcanoes. Mount Semeru on Java rises to 3,676 m.

Source: NASA Earth Observatory, Sumatra Subduction Zone · Indonesia Travel, Bromo Tengger Semeru National Park

Vietnam An S-shaped country · pho noodles and the ao dai. Thailand The land of smiles · elephants and temples. Indonesia One of the countries with the most islands in the world · Bali.

South Asia

Himalayan foothills, plains and Deccan Plateau

The world’s highest mountains, the Himalayas, line the north of South Asia. Big rivers flowing from them built wide plains. The south is a huge peninsula reaching into the ocean.

From north to south lie the Himalayas, the plains of the Indus, Ganges and Brahmaputra, the Deccan Plateau of the Indian peninsula and offshore islands.

Himalayas on the northern edge

The Himalayas run about 2,500 km across Nepal, Bhutan and northern India. They began rising some 50 million years ago as the Indian plate pushed into Eurasia, and they are still growing.

Source: National Geographic Society, Asia: Physical Geography

River plains and deltas

The Indus flows to the Arabian Sea, while the Ganges and Brahmaputra reach the Bay of Bengal. Where they meet, around Bangladesh, they form one of the world’s largest deltas.

Source: NASA MODIS, Indus River · NASA Earth Observatory, The Braided Brahmaputra

The Deccan Plateau

The Deccan Plateau, built partly from ancient lava flows, fills much of the Indian peninsula. The island of Sri Lanka lies off its southern tip.

Source: National Geographic Society, Asia: Physical Geography · IUGS, Deccan Traps

India The land of the Taj Mahal and curry.

Central Asia

Mountains between steppe and desert

Central Asia lies very far from any ocean. It has wide grasslands and deserts, and tall snowy mountains rise in the east.

Far from the sea, the region is dry, with big differences between summer and winter. Northern steppe, central deserts and the eastern Tien Shan and Pamir mountains follow one another.

The Kazakh steppe

Northern Kazakhstan has open grassland with few trees, called steppe, dotted with lakes. Migrating birds stop to rest here.

Source: UNESCO, Saryarka - Steppe and Lakes of Northern Kazakhstan

The Tien Shan

The Tien Shan stretches about 2,400 km across Kyrgyzstan, Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan and China. Snow and glacier meltwater feed rivers and farmland in the dry lands below.

Source: National Geographic Society, Asia: Physical Geography · UNESCO, Western Tien-Shan

The landlocked Caspian

The Caspian, on Central Asia’s western edge, is called a sea but is Earth’s largest landlocked body of water. Rivers such as the Volga flow in, but no natural channel leads out to the ocean.

Source: NASA Earth Observatory, Caspian Sea · NASA Earth Observatory, Volga Delta and the Caspian Sea

Uzbekistan The heart of the Silk Road · Samarkand and the Registan.

West Asia

Rivers between deserts and mountains

West Asia has wide, hot and dry deserts. High mountain ranges stand beside them. Where two great rivers flow, the land turns green.

The region combines the deserts of the Arabian Peninsula, the mountains and plateaus of Iran, and the lowlands along the Tigris and Euphrates.

Deserts of Arabia

The Rub’ al Khali in the south of the Arabian Peninsula is an endless sea of sand larger than France. Mountains rise along the Red Sea on the peninsula’s west.

Source: National Geographic Society, Asia: Physical Geography · Saudipedia, Al-Rub’ Al-Khali (Empty Quarter) Desert

Iran’s Zagros Mountains

Iran’s Zagros Mountains formed as rock layers were squeezed and folded like wrinkles. Long ridges and valleys run side by side.

Source: NASA Earth Observatory, Ridges and Valleys of the Zagros Mountains

Land between two rivers

The Tigris and Euphrates cross the lowlands of Iraq toward the Persian Gulf. Near their mouths, broad reed marshes stand out against the surrounding desert.

Source: NASA Earth Observatory, The Mesopotamian Marshes

Saudi Arabia A country of wide deserts and oases · camels.
